# Reviewer notes - Nashville Trade Permits brief (2026-11-07)

**Thesis (Standards Section 13.1, written BEFORE the analysis ran, 2026-11-06 evening, before any statistic was computed):** Nashville's construction story does not end at the building permit: the trade permits record the wiring, plumbing, and mechanical work that follows the buildings, they split unevenly across the trades, and their volume and stated contract value concentrate in a few council districts rather than spreading evenly across the city.

If the analysis does not support this thesis, the thesis changes and the change is recorded here; the numbers never change to fit it.

**Thesis revision (recorded after the pre-flight audit and stats run, before drafting):** the original thesis's concentration clause failed on the data. Trade permit VOLUME does not concentrate in a few districts: after normalizing the district field's mixed zero-padded and bare spellings, the largest district (19) holds 5.9 percent of district-known permits, the top-to-median district ratio is 2.2 (the beer register's was 11.4), and all 35 districts appear. Stated VALUE cannot be totaled honestly at all: Contract_Value repeats the parent project's stated value across that project's trade permits (19,625 rows repeat a (parcel, value) pair), so value concentration by district is not measurable from sums. What the data does support, and the revised thesis: **Nashville's trade permits are the fast, flat underlayer of its construction system: 111,321 entered in three years (about 100 a day), 87.1 percent issued the same day they were entered while the median building permit waited 29 days, spread across all 35 council districts with no district holding even 6 percent of them, and 43.2 percent of the largest trade, electrical, is service releases and reconnects on the existing city rather than new wiring. Where concentration does exist, it is at the top of the project scale: 117 of the 141 distinct parcels whose trade permits state a project value of $10 million or more sit in District 19.**

**Thesis revision 2 (recorded after the Standards Section 11 adversarial review, before publication):** the review refuted the "on the existing city rather than new wiring" characterization. Service releases (14,064, the family's largest class) mostly track permitted construction: 76.1 percent sit on a parcel holding an issued building permit in the companion window and 45.6 percent of their purpose statements contain "new"; the reconnect classes (8,637) mostly do not (9.4 to 35.5 percent). The denominator in the speed clause was also tightened (87.1 percent is of dated permits, not of all 111,321). Final thesis as published: **Nashville's trade permits are the fast, flat underlayer of its construction system: 111,321 entered in three years (about 100 a day), 87.1 percent of dated permits issued the same day they were entered while the median building permit waited 29 days, spread across all 35 council districts with no district holding even 6 percent of them, and 43.2 percent of the largest trade, electrical, is service-connection paperwork rather than wiring permits - releases that mostly track permitted construction and reconnects that mostly serve addresses without it. Where concentration does exist, it is at the top of the project scale: 117 of the 141 distinct parcels whose trade permits state a project value of $10 million or more sit in District 19.**

Claims most likely to be challenged:
- The 87.1 percent same-day figure. Its denominator is the 107,210 dated records with a non-negative gap, not all 111,321 records; the summary, hero, README, and social copy now carry the dated population. As a share of all entered permits the same-day count is 83.9 percent. The adversarial review caught the looser framing; the fix is recorded in verification.md.
- The service-connection characterization. The count (22,701; 43.2 percent) is exact, but the first draft's gloss ("existing city, not new wiring") was refuted by the Purpose text and the parcel join; the published brief splits the family by evidence instead (releases track construction; reconnects mostly do not).
- The Contract_Value treatment. The brief asserts behavior (project value repeated per permit) while the metadata basis is undefined; the text now says exactly that, and Metro is asked to define the field. A reader who believes Contract_Value is per-trade will still dispute the no-summing rule; the repetition counts (19,625 same-parcel repeats; one $53,809,905 statement on at least six permits) are printed so the dispute is checkable.
- The 29-day building comparison. Different populations (all dated trade records vs issued building permits only); labeled in the brief body, and the beer contrast now carries its own population note plus the dated-only sensitivity (District 19 at 5.9 percent either way).

Methods checks that could not be completed:
- Case_Status semantics: the layer's public metadata does not define DONE vs ISSUED (or any status), and no agency definition was available to this build; statuses are reported as recorded, with the issued-date cross-tab printed in the audit.
- Why the snapshot's entries end October 16, 2026 (view refresh lag vs a genuine pause) cannot be determined from the service; the brief states the snapshot's own end date and claims nothing past it.
- Whether a service release links to a parent permit inside Metro's source system: not visible in the public view; the brief's "Who can act" section asks for the link to be published rather than assuming it.

Findings that surprised us:
- The largest stated value in the register ($98,984,000, a District 19 gas/mechanical permit) carries a Purpose statement that opens with an internal payment-handling note (a check left with a named staffer). Staff working text is sitting in a public field; the brief does not name the staffer.
- The slowest permit classes in the register are the tiny photovoltaic ones (median gaps of 13 to 15 days across 143 dated permits), not any high-volume class.
- District 19's stated-value footprint, computed the naive way, would have read as 72.6 percent of a $26.8 billion total - an artifact of project values repeated per permit. The deduplicated footprint (117 of 141 large-project parcels) is the real concentration story, and it is stronger for being smaller.
- The district field's mixed zero-padding ("05" vs "5" on 776 rows) would have manufactured five phantom one-digit districts and understated District 2 by 342 permits.
